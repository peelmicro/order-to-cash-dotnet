## Notes for the gate

- **Row 1 is the only decision that needs you.** It is a genuine .NET consequence: #7 forked a controller and paid nothing because its framework supplied the four behaviours; #8's are hand-built and separately armed, so forking would duplicate them into an unguarded copy. The alternative — a second responder class — is written up and rejected in `design.md` §4.1 and §14, and the narrower option (rename only the responder, leave the two mappers credit-named while they map invoice facts and invoice errors) is worse for the same reason it looks smaller: it leaves two files whose names assert something false, which is what row 17 is fixing elsewhere in this same pass.
- **Rows 2 and 4 are the two worth the most reading time even though neither is GATE.** Row 2 is a documented deviation from a `specs/shared/` rule; the brief asked for it to be re-derived rather than inherited, and `design.md` §7.4 does that in four itemised points, three of which say the deviation is *cheaper* in #8 and one of which prices what #8 loses (the `tx` parameter's visibility, replaced by `ValidateScopes = true` and a forced-rollback test). Row 4 is this feature's only divergence from #7's bytes; it follows a ratified rule rather than making a new decision, which is why it is not GATE, but it changes what the Gateway and the n8n robot must read and it is carried in the hand-over.
- **#7 had zero gate rows on this feature and was still rejected — on two guards, not on behaviour.** Both are written into `tasks.md` as prevention: `N2`/`N10` (prove placement by observing the entry, never the residue — `F1`) and `N1` (0 of 60 boxes ticked — the preamble makes ticking and honest rewording an explicit close criterion). Its `N3`, `N4` and `N7` are likewise pre-empted at rows 9, 10 and 11.
- **The ledger has 32 rows from 30 enumerated boundaries**, and its preamble names a pattern rather than only listing rows: **four** of them (`L4`, `L20`, `L23`, and feature 19's `L27` before them) exist because #7's property came from TypeScript being **structurally** typed. In a nominal type system each of those has to be *constructed* — by a closed hierarchy, by a container lifetime rule, by an explicit port — and a constructed property needs a guard where a structural one needed none. That question, *"was the thing that made this correct over there the type system?"*, is invisible in the code being copied, and phases 11–13 port three more services from the same source.
- **39 of 59 tasks carry the arming flag.** That is deliberately high. The two features that shipped unguarded countable claims both armed 100 % of their **flagged** tasks; the defect each time was a countable claim in an unflagged task. Every task below whose prose says *exactly one*, *never*, *no*, *equals*, *in this order* or *all six* carries ⚑, and the preamble tells the implementer to arm any it finds that does not and to say so.

## Gate ruling (2026-09-06)

**Row 1 APPROVED as recommended.** Extend the one `BackgroundService` to five subjects and rename the four credit-scoped types service-scoped across 14 files; do **not** fork a second responder.

The reasoning the gate accepted, restated so it is not re-litigated in review: #7 declared a second controller and paid nothing for it, because NestJS supplied concurrency, per-request DI scope and graceful shutdown. **#8 hand-builds all three inside the existing responder and each is separately armed** — a distinct DI scope per request, the drain in both halves, and individually-awaited fault isolation, that last one closed only one feature ago as backlog id 50. A second responder class would fork four armed behaviours into an unguarded copy, which is the shape the human's standing instruction of 2026-09-05 was given against. `CLAUDE.md`'s non-negotiable is **one `BackgroundService` per transport**, and Billing's RPC transport is one.

Task `E4`'s condition is the part that makes the rename safe and must not be softened: the three existing responder test files are green afterwards **with no assertion changed**.

### Two rows the spec resolved without asking, and the gate endorses both

**Row 2 — the two-aggregate transaction.** The brief required this to be re-derived against #8's code rather than inherited on #7's citation, and `design.md` §7.4 does it in four itemised points: three find the deviation *cheaper* here (there is no `tx` to thread), one prices what #8 loses and names its replacement. That is the standard for inheriting a rule-breaking deviation — evidence from this repository, not a citation from the other one.

**Row 4 — `paidAt` omitted where #7 emitted `null`.** Correctly not made a gate row. The nulls-omitted rule was ratified at a human gate and a per-property exception would be **the first exception to a rule whose entire value is that there are none**. Following a ratified rule is not a decision to escalate; escalating it would have asked the human to re-ratify their own rule. Both shapes validate against the contract and the payload parity claim is semantic. Carried in the hand-over because it changes what the Gateway and the n8n workflows read.
